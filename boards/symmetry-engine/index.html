A symmetry is a move that leaves a shape looking exactly the same. Pick a shape, then turn it or flip it. The faint outline shows where it started. If the shape lands on its own outline, you have found a symmetry. Can you find them all?

The rule behind it: with n turns and no mirrors there are n symmetries (the cyclic group Cn). Add mirrors and there are 2n (the dihedral group Dn). Leonardo da Vinci noticed that every pattern around a single point is one of these.

Seven ways to walk

A frieze repeats along a strip: a border, a railing, a line of footprints. However inventive the design, there are only seven kinds. John Conway named them after the footprints you would leave: hop, step, sidle, jump and their spinning versions. Draw in the strip and watch your mark repeat.

Lab 6 · VIP

Why exactly 17? Shop for a wallpaper

John Conway found a price list for symmetry. Every wallpaper pattern is described by a short code, and every symbol in the code has a price. His "magic theorem": a pattern can tile the plane exactly when its code costs $2. Spend exactly $2 in every possible way and you find all 17 wallpapers, no more and no fewer.

The price list

Turning points are written as numbers (a 4 means a quarter-turn centre). Mirrors are a star *. Numbers after a star are corners where mirrors meet. A glide is a cross x. A plain repeat is an o.
Your basket is empty: $0

Free example: the snowflake wallpaper p6m has code *632: a star ($1) plus corners 6, 3 and 2 ($5/12 + $1/3 + $1/4). Total: exactly $2.

The forbidden five

Wallpapers can turn by a half, a third, a quarter or a sixth, but never by a fifth. Try it: turn a grid of points about one of its points and see whether it lands on itself. The reason is a one-line proof: the turn must move grid points to grid points, so 2 cos(360°/n) has to be a whole number, which only happens for n = 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6.

But nature found a way

A Penrose tiling has a centre with perfect five-fold symmetry, yet it never repeats: slide it anywhere and it does not land on itself. In 1982 Dan Shechtman found crystals built like this, quasicrystals, and won the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Escher's infinite kaleidoscope

Three mirrors meeting at angles of 180°/p, 180°/q and 90° make a kaleidoscope. When 1/p + 1/q = 1/2 the mirrors tile the flat plane. When 1/p + 1/q is smaller, there is too much room: the copies shrink towards the rim of a disc and never end. This is the hyperbolic plane, the geometry M. C. Escher used for his Circle Limit prints. Two colours show the trick: every mirror swaps black and white.

How symmetric is it?

Real things are almost symmetric: faces, leaves, sensor readings, a spinning machine. The Symmetry Index measures how far something is from perfect balance, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: it is exactly 1 when every part is equal and falls as the parts drift apart.

Six readings x₁…x₆. Symmetry Index = (x₁ + … + x₆)² ÷ (6 · (x₁² + … + x₆²)). Drag the readings, watch the index, and see which departures matter most.
