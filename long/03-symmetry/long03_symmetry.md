## 0 · Hook (draft)

> *Visual: snowflake rotating and flipping unchanged; a body outline with heart on the left; everything dissolving to empty light*

**VO:** Here is a snowflake. Turn it by a sixth of a turn, and nothing changes. Flip it over, and nothing changes. It is one of the most symmetric things in nature.

**VO:** Here is you. Your heart sits on the left. Your face is a little different on each side. And the molecules in your body all twist the same way.

**VO:** Here is the strange part. If the universe had kept every symmetry it started with, there would be no snowflake and no you. Nothing at all. Everything you see exists because, again and again, a perfect symmetry broke.

**VO:** Today we follow those breaks, from the first second of the universe to the moment your heart moved to the left. Five breaks, and at the end of them, you.

---

## 1 · What symmetry is (draft)

> *Visual: a square turning and flipping, 8 moves counted; a circle; reflection, rotation, translation demos; blank circle*

**VO:** First, what does symmetry actually mean? Mathematicians have a beautifully simple answer. A symmetry is something you can do to an object that leaves it looking exactly the same.

**VO:** Take a square. Turn it a quarter turn: same square. Flip it across a diagonal: same square. Count every move that works and you find eight. A circle has infinitely many: turn it by any angle at all.

**VO:** Three kinds of move do almost all the work. Reflection, a mirror. Rotation, a turn. And translation, a slide, like a wallpaper pattern that repeats forever.

**VO:** Notice something. The more symmetric a thing is, the less there is to say about it. A blank circle has the most symmetry and the least information. Hold on to that idea. It is the key to the whole story.

---

## 2 · Symmetry in nature (draft)

> *Visual: hexagonal lattice growing into a snowflake; starfish (and bilateral larva); flower; bilateral mirror line on animals*

**VO:** Nature is full of it. A snowflake has six arms because water molecules lock together into a hexagonal lattice. And all six arms grow through the same changing air at the same moment, so they nearly match.

**VO:** A starfish has five-fold symmetry, although, as a larva, it had a left and a right side, just like us. A daisy, a sunflower, an orange cut in half: rotations again.

**VO:** And look at almost any animal: a butterfly, a fish, a tiger, you. One mirror line down the middle. Biologists call it bilateral symmetry, and over ninety-eight percent of known animal species share it.

**VO:** Why is nature so symmetric? Because the rules behind it are. In 1894 Pierre Curie made a simple point: whatever symmetry the causes have shows up in the effects. So if something is lopsided, the lopsidedness had to come from somewhere.

**VO:** Which raises the real question. If the laws are symmetric, where does all the asymmetry come from?

---

## 3 · The laws are symmetric (draft)

> *Visual: one throw repeated today/tomorrow, slid, turned; three symmetry->conservation cards*

**VO:** In 1918 the mathematician Emmy Noether proved what many physicists call one of the most beautiful results in science.

**VO:** Do an experiment today, then the same experiment tomorrow. You get the same result, because the laws of physics do not change with time. That is a symmetry. Noether proved that this symmetry is exactly why energy is conserved.

**VO:** Slide the experiment across the room: same laws, and that is why momentum is conserved. Turn it to face another way: same laws, and that is why angular momentum, spin, is conserved.

**VO:** Every symmetry of the laws comes with something that can never be created or destroyed. Symmetry is not decoration. It is the deepest rule in physics.

**VO:** But a world that kept every symmetry would be very dull. Let's see what happens when one breaks.

---

## 4 · Break one: matter beats antimatter (draft)

> *Visual: particle/antiparticle pairs annihilating into light; a billion vs a billion and one counter; leftovers become stars*

**VO:** Break one happened in the first fraction of a second. The young universe made matter and antimatter in pairs: a perfectly symmetric deal. When a particle meets its antiparticle, both vanish into light.

**VO:** If the deal had been perfect, every particle would have found its partner, and the universe would be nothing but light. No atoms, no stars, nothing.

**VO:** It wasn't perfect. For roughly every billion particles of antimatter, there was about one extra particle of matter. The billions annihilated. The extra one survived. Every galaxy, every star, every atom in your body is made of those leftovers.

**VO:** Physicists first caught nature playing favourites in 1964, in particles called kaons. Exactly how the universe tipped the balance is still one of the biggest open questions in physics.

---

## 5 · Break two: a smooth universe gets lumpy (draft)

> *Visual: a smooth disc of old light with tiny ripples, exaggerated; lumps growing under gravity into a cosmic web*

**VO:** Break two. Look at the oldest light there is, released about three hundred and eighty thousand years after the Big Bang. It is almost perfectly smooth: the same temperature in every direction.

**VO:** Almost. The differences are about one part in a hundred thousand, like ripples a hundredth of a millimetre high on a pond a metre deep.

**VO:** Gravity loves a lump. A slightly denser spot pulls in a little more, which makes it denser still. Over billions of years those tiny ripples grew into galaxies, stars and planets. A perfectly smooth universe would have stayed smooth forever.

---

## 6 · Break three: life picks a hand (draft)

> *Visual: two hands; Pasteur crystals sorted; polarised light turning; amino acid L vs D; right-handed helix*

**VO:** Break three. Hold up your hands. They are mirror images, but you cannot turn one into the other. Many molecules are like that: they come in a left-handed and a right-handed version.

**VO:** In 1848 a young Louis Pasteur looked at crystals under a microscope and saw two shapes, mirror images of each other. He separated them by hand, with tweezers. Dissolved in water, one turned polarised light one way, and the other turned it the opposite way, by exactly the same amount.

**VO:** Ordinary chemistry makes both hands in equal amounts. Life does not. Almost every amino acid in your proteins is left-handed. The sugars in your DNA are right-handed, and the double helix twists to the right.

**VO:** Life chose one hand and stuck with it. How that first choice was made is still an open question. But without it, the parts of a cell could not fit together, like trying to shake a right hand with a left.

---

## 7 · Break four: from a ball to a body (draft)

> *Visual: a uniform field breaking into Turing spots and stripes; ball cell -> axes; Ikaria-like worm and tracks*

**VO:** Break four. You began as a single cell, roughly a ball. A ball looks the same from every direction. So how does it decide where the head goes?

**VO:** In 1952 Alan Turing, the codebreaker, wrote a paper about exactly this. He showed that an even mix of chemicals that react and spread can become unstable, precisely because they spread, and settle all by itself into spots and stripes. Tiny random differences get amplified, just like the lumps in the early universe.

**VO:** Processes like that, together with cues already in the egg, lay out a body: head and tail, back and belly. About five hundred and fifty-five million years ago, some of the first animals built like this, small worm-like creatures, crawled across the sea floor and left their tracks. A front, a back, and one mirror line down the middle. That body plan leads all the way to you.

---

## 8 · Break five: the heart moves left (draft)

> *Visual: the node pit with tilted spinning cilia pushing flow left; heart moving to the left*

**VO:** But you are not quite mirror symmetric either. Your heart is on the left, your liver on the right. Who decided?

**VO:** In the early embryo there is a tiny pit called the node, lined with hair-like cilia. They all spin clockwise, seen from below, and because they tilt backwards, they push the fluid towards the left. Japanese scientists filmed it happening in 1998. That gentle current, in a pit smaller than a millimetre, is the first moment your body knows its left from its right.

**VO:** Every so often the flow goes wrong, and a person is born with their organs mirrored. It happens to roughly one person in ten thousand, and many never find out.

---

## 9 · The chain (draft)

> *Visual: the chain of five breaks as a timeline from Big Bang to you*

**VO:** So here is the chain. The laws of physics are symmetric. Matter beat antimatter by one in a billion. The smooth universe got lumpy and made stars. Life picked a hand. A round cell picked a head and a tail. And a whirl of cilia put your heart on the left.

**VO:** Symmetry gives the world its rules. Breaking it gives the world its stories. You are made of five broken symmetries.
