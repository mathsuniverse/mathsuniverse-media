# The monotile problem, stated precisely

Written 1 Nov 2026 after Razvan's remark: "the covering problem is a symmetry group problem with a
finite number of generators. Maybe only 2? We have to state it mathematically in full before we look at proofs."

## 1. Definitions

- **Tile.** A compact set T ⊂ R² that is a closed topological disc (here: a polygon).
- **E(2)** is the group of isometries of the plane (translations, rotations, reflections, glides).
- **Tiling by T.** A family 𝒯 = { gᵢT : i ∈ ℕ }, gᵢ ∈ E(2), with
  (i) ⋃ᵢ gᵢT = R²  (covering) and (ii) int(gᵢT) ∩ int(gⱼT) = ∅ for i ≠ j  (packing).
  (On a grid, the gᵢ are restricted to symmetries of the grid; then everything is combinatorial.)
- **Symmetry group of a tiling.** Sym(𝒯) = { g ∈ E(2) : g𝒯 = 𝒯 }.
- **Periodic.** 𝒯 is periodic if Sym(𝒯) contains two linearly independent translations.
  Equivalently Sym(𝒯) is a discrete cocompact group, i.e. one of the 17 wallpaper groups.
- **Aperiodic monotile.** T admits at least one tiling, and no tiling by T is periodic.
  (Strongly: no tiling by T has any non-trivial translational symmetry. The hat is strongly aperiodic.)

## 2. Where the group picture is exactly right: periodic tilings

If 𝒯 is periodic, Γ = Sym(𝒯) is a crystallographic group, finitely generated
(a wallpaper group needs between 2 generators, for p1 (two translations), and 4, e.g. pmm (four reflections)).
If moreover Γ acts transitively on the tiles (an **isohedral** tiling), then 𝒯 = Γ·T is the orbit of
one tile, and the generators can be read off the tile itself:

- each edge segment e of T is matched to a neighbour g_e T; the maps g_e generate Γ;
- the vertices give the relations (Poincaré's polygon theorem: the products of the g_e around each vertex cycle are the identity or a rotation of finite order);
- there are exactly 81 types of isohedral tiling (93 counting marked tiles; Grünbaum–Shephard), and deciding whether a polygon admits one is a finite check.

So for periodic tilings, "covering = a group with finitely many generators" is a theorem, and the
generators are the edge-pairings of the tile. Most tiles that tile at all are of this kind.
Anisohedral tiles (Heesch 1935) tile periodically but only with several tile orbits, i.e. Γ acts
with k > 1 orbits on the tiles: still a finitely generated group, now on a k-tile block.

## 3. What aperiodicity means in this language

T is an aperiodic monotile ⇔ **no** tiling by T has a cocompact symmetry group.
For the hat, Sym(𝒯) is finite (often trivial) for every hat tiling 𝒯.
So the finite-generator description must fail, and the question is what replaces it.

**What replaces it: a single non-isometric "generator", the inflation.**
The hat's tilings are organised by finitely many clusters (the metatiles H, T, P, F) and a
substitution σ: each metatile, scaled by a factor λ > 1 (λ = φ² for the hat family, φ the golden
ratio), is exactly covered by copies of the metatiles. Every hat tiling is the limit of σⁿ applied
to a seed. Here λ = φ², not φ: the metatile edge vectors scale by the dominant eigenvalue of the
substitution matrix, which is φ⁴ = (7+3√5)/2 ≈ 6.85, so lengths scale by its square root, φ².

So the hat is described by **finite local data + one expanding map**, instead of
**finite local data + a group of isometries**. In the language of dynamics: the set Ω of all tilings
by T (the hull) is compact; R² acts on it by translation; for a periodic tile Ω contains a torus,
while for the hat, σ is a homeomorphism Ω → Ω that expands by λ.

The precise link is **Solomyak's theorem** (1998): for a self-affine tiling, the tiling is
non-periodic ⇔ σ is injective on the hull (the decomposition into supertiles is unique,
"recognizability"). This is the bridge from "finitely many local rules" to "never periodic".

So Razvan's "maybe only 2" has a precise form:
- periodic world: a lattice generated by **2 translations** (plus point symmetries);
- aperiodic (hat) world: the translations are replaced by **1 inflation** σ acting on finitely many cluster types.

## 4. The search problem, stated as logic

For a grid tile T and a budget k ∈ ℕ:
- D_k(T): "copies of T cover the disc of radius k" (decidable, finite);
- P_k(T): "T tiles a torus with ≤ k tiles in a fundamental domain" (decidable, finite).

Then
- T tiles the plane ⇔ ∀k D_k(T)                  (extension theorem / compactness)
- T admits a periodic tiling ⇔ ∃k P_k(T)
- **T is an aperiodic monotile ⇔ ∀k ( D_k(T) ∧ ¬P_k(T) )**

The set of aperiodic monotiles is therefore co-recursively-enumerable (a Π₁ property): it can be
refuted by a finite certificate (a stuck disc, or a torus) but never confirmed by computation alone.
This is the same logical shape as Goldbach's conjecture. Collatz ("every n reaches 1") is Π₂, one
level higher. Its generalisations are undecidable (Conway 1972), just as the general tiling problem is
for sets of tiles (Berger 1966). For a single tile in the plane, decidability is open.

## 5. What a proof for a new candidate must contain

1. **Existence:** T tiles the plane (e.g. by exhibiting a substitution that generates arbitrarily large patches).
2. **Forced clusters:** in every tiling by T, the tiles group into finitely many cluster types M₁…M_m.
   This is a finite, computer-checkable enumeration of all possible neighbourhoods (coronas) of T.
3. **Substitution:** the clusters satisfy an inflation rule σ with factor λ > 1, and the composition
   into clusters is again forced at the next level.
4. **Recognizability ⇒ non-periodicity:** the decomposition is unique, so by Solomyak's theorem
   (or a direct argument: a period would survive inflation and become arbitrarily small), no tiling is periodic.

Steps 2 and 3 are where computation helps. Steps 1–4 together are what the hat paper does
(Smith, Myers, Kaplan, Goodman-Strauss 2023, arXiv 2303.10798, sections 4–6), plus a second, independent proof.

## 6. Consequence for the search

The useful question is not "which shape?" but **"which substitution?"**:
look for small systems (m cluster types, inflation λ, edge-matching data) that can be realised by
copies of a single tile. A shape found that way comes with steps 2–4 already outlined.

## 7. What is known (summary, 1 Nov 2026)
- Classified: the 17 wallpaper groups; isohedral tilings (81 types, 93 marked); convex tiles
  (all triangles and quadrilaterals; 3 hexagon families, Reinhardt 1918; 15 pentagon families, list complete, Rao 2017;
  no convex n ≥ 7). All convex tilers tile periodically, so an aperiodic monotile must be non-convex.
- Not classified: tiles in general; no general algorithm known for "does T tile?" for one tile.
- Aperiodic sets: Berger 1966 (20,426 Wang tiles), Robinson (6), Penrose (2), minimum Wang set 11 (Jeandel–Rao 2015).
- Aperiodic monotiles: Socolar–Taylor 2010 (needs markings or a disconnected tile); the hat family Tile(a,b) and the
  Spectre (2023). Every known clean aperiodic monotile is in this one family.
- Each aperiodic monotile admits uncountably many tilings, all locally indistinguishable.

## 8. Rational vs irrational (Razvan, 1 Nov: "aperiodic = irrational, periodic = rational")

- Theorem (standard): in a periodic tiling every tile-type frequency is rational (count in a fundamental
  domain / its size). Hence if a tile forces an irrational frequency in every tiling, it is aperiodic.
- Checked numerically on our own patches (1 Nov 2026):
  hat, unreflected:reflected = 6.682 (169), 6.864 (1,156), 6.8504 (7,921), 6.8543 (54,289) → φ⁴ = 6.8541;
  Penrose P2, kites:darts = 1.660, 1.650, 1.633, 1.627 (deflation depth 5–8) → φ = 1.6180.
- The converse is false: e.g. the chair tiling (inflation 2) is non-periodic with rational frequencies.
- Exact version for cut-and-project sets: a slice of a lattice at rational slope is periodic, at irrational slope
  quasiperiodic (1D: Sturmian words; Fibonacci word slope 1/φ; Penrose = slice of Z⁵).
- Use in the search: for any surviving candidate, measure tile-type and orientation frequencies on large patches;
  convergence to a quadratic irrational (φ, √2, √3, ...) is evidence of an inflation hierarchy with a Pisot factor.

## 9. Target: metallic-mean monotiles (Razvan, 1 Nov)

Metallic means λ_n are the positive roots of x² = n·x + 1, all Pisot units:
- n = 1, golden φ = 1.618: Penrose (1974), 5-fold; also the hat family's hierarchy (inflation φ², mirror ratio φ⁴).
- n = 2, silver 1+√2 = 2.414: Ammann–Beenker (squares + 45° rhombs), 8-fold.
- n = 3, bronze (3+√13)/2 = 3.303: bronze-mean hexagonal quasicrystal (Dotera, Bekku, Ziherl, Nature Materials 16, 987–992, 2017).
All known metallic-mean quasicrystal tilings use several prototiles. The only clean aperiodic monotiles
(hat family, Spectre) are golden. Open question for us: does a single tile exist whose forced hierarchy is
silver (or bronze)? If so, the hat is the n = 1 member of a family of metallic monotiles.
Search plan: 45° grids (tetrakis square) for silver, hexagonal grids for bronze; structure-first: try to force
copies of one tile into the clusters of the known silver/bronze substitutions; signature: frequency ratios → λ_n powers.

## 10. Conjecture (Razvan, 1 Nov): "all aperiodic orders relate to the metallic family"

**True core: crystallographic restriction.** A rotation by θ has characteristic polynomial x² − 2cos θ·x + 1.
A lattice symmetry needs integer trace 2cos θ, so only orders 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 are possible in periodic patterns.
Other orders force an irrational trace from Q(cos 2π/n): 5/10-fold → 1/φ (golden: Penrose); 8-fold → √2
(silver 1+√2: Ammann–Beenker); 12-fold → √3 (inflation 2+√3); 7-fold → cubic. So for non-crystallographic
symmetry the rotation's characteristic equation fixes the irrational (the slope of the cut-and-project slice).

**False as stated (counterexamples):** chair tiling (aperiodic, inflation 2, rational frequencies, only 4-fold);
pinwheel tiling (Conway–Radin, inflation √5, not Pisot, infinitely many orientations); 12-fold tilings (2+√3, root of
x²−4x+1, not metallic). Kenyon (1996): every complex Perron number is the expansion factor of some self-similar
plane tiling, a class far larger than the metallic means.

**Refined conjecture to test:** metallic means are roots of x² − n x − 1; their siblings are roots of
x² − n x + 1 (n ≥ 3), e.g. φ² (n = 3: the hat's inflation) and 2+√3 (n = 4). Together these are all quadratic
Pisot units. Conjecture: every aperiodic monotile whose tilings have pure point diffraction has a Pisot inflation
factor; in the quadratic case it is a metallic mean or a sibling. The Pisot part is supported by Solomyak's theory
of pure point spectrum for self-similar tilings; the monotile restriction is open. Next members to look for: silver, bronze.

## 11. Lifting: tilings as projections (Razvan, 1 Nov: "a projection from higher dimensional space ... maybe a Gauss map")

**Cut-and-project.** Lattice L ⊂ Rᴺ (e.g. Z⁵ for Penrose); split Rᴺ = E ⊕ E⊥ with E the 2D physical plane;
window W ⊂ E⊥; vertex set Λ = { π_E(x) : x ∈ L, π_⊥(x) ∈ W }.
**The "angle"** is the point E ∈ Gr(2, N), the Grassmannian of 2-planes (generalising the Gauss map, which takes values in
Gr(1,3) = the sphere of normals up to sign). Literal Gauss-map version: lift a tiling to a stepped surface in Zᴺ
(each edge = a unit lattice step); its asymptotic tangent plane is a point of Gr(2,N).
**Two parameters:** slope E (rational → periodic, irrational → quasiperiodic) and offset (a point of the torus Rᴺ/L,
selecting the individual tiling; uncountably many).
**The hat is a projection:** Baake, Gähler, Mazáč, Sadun, "Dynamics and topology of the Hat family of tilings" (arXiv 2305.05639):
control points of the CAP tiling form a cut-and-project set with internal space R², total window a regular hexagon,
subwindow boundaries partly Koch-type fractals (dimension ≈ 1.368); all Hat-family tilings are topologically conjugate up to
linear maps (turtle, chevron, hat, comet covered); the Spectre is not covered.
**Not all aperiodic orders are Euclidean projections:** chair (2-adic internal space), pinwheel (not a model set);
Pisot-type pure point orders are the expected model sets (Pisot conjecture).
**Search route C (projection-first):** choose an irrational plane (silver: 8-fold, bronze: hexagonal) and a window, project,
then look for a single shape whose copies merge the resulting tiles (as the hat merges its metatiles).

## 12. The Pisot family as the organising theory

**Definitions.** λ > 1 is a Pisot number if it is an algebraic integer whose other conjugates all have |·| < 1
(Salem: conjugates ≤ 1 with one on the unit circle). A Pisot unit has norm ±1.

**Key theorems (standard, to be cited precisely in any write-up):**
1. Meyer (1972): if Λ is a Meyer set (e.g. a cut-and-project set) and λΛ ⊆ Λ with λ > 1, then λ is Pisot or Salem.
   So projection + self-similarity ⇒ Pisot/Salem inflation.
2. Thurston, Kenyon (1996): expansion factors of self-similar plane tilings are exactly the complex Perron numbers
   (a much larger class); so non-Pisot inflations exist (pinwheel, λ = √5, conjugate −√5) but are not model sets.
3. Solomyak and the Pisot substitution programme: for Pisot inflations, pure point diffraction ⇔ model-set
   structure (proved in many cases, conjectured in general: the Pisot conjecture).
4. Internal space: unimodular (unit) Pisot inflations lead to Euclidean internal spaces (Rauzy-fractal windows);
   non-unit ones need p-adic factors from the primes dividing the norm.
   Example: the chair, λ = 2 (Pisot, norm 2) has a 2-adic internal space; the hat, λ = φ² (unit) has internal space R².

**So the earlier "counterexamples" fit:** chair = integer Pisot, non-unit → p-adic; pinwheel = non-Pisot → no projection;
12-fold = 2+√3, a quadratic Pisot unit (sibling).

**Quadratic Pisot units (computed):** roots of x² − n x − 1 (metallic, n ≥ 1) and x² − n x + 1 (siblings, n ≥ 3):
1.6180 golden (n=1), 2.4142 silver (n=2), 2.6180 = φ² (sibling n=3: the hat), 3.3028 bronze (n=3),
3.7321 = 2+√3 (sibling n=4: 12-fold), 4.2361 = φ³ (n=4), 4.7913 (sibling n=5), 5.1926 (n=5),
5.8284 = (1+√2)² (sibling n=6), 6.1623 (n=6). Siblings with n = m² + 2 are squares of metallic means.

**Working conjecture (refined):** an aperiodic monotile whose tilings are pure point (quasicrystal-like) has a Pisot
inflation. If that is a unit, the window is Euclidean and the order is "metallic-type"; the quadratic cases are
exactly the list above. Known monotiles realise only the golden field Q(√5) (hat: φ²). Open: Q(√2) (silver), Q(√13) (bronze),
Q(√3) (12-fold).

## 13. Testing the silver / bronze / 12-fold question (1 Nov 2026, evening)

**Correction to section 12.** The Spectre is NOT golden. Its area inflation is 4+√15 ≈ 7.873 (root of x² − 8x + 1),
field Q(√15), internal space R⁴, Rauzy-fractal windows (Baake, Gähler, Mazáč, Sadun, arXiv 2411.15503).
The hat's area inflation is φ⁴ ≈ 6.854 (root of x² − 7x + 1); measured here from hat counts 4, 25, 169, 1156, 7921
(ratios 6.25, 6.76, 6.840, 6.852 → 6.854). So the two known monotile families realise two fields, Q(√5) and Q(√15),
and both area factors are sibling Pisot units (norm +1, n = 7 and n = 8). Socolar–Taylor: λ = 2 (rational, 2-adic).

**Sibling unit in each target field** (x² − n x + 1, field Q(√(n² − 4))):
Q(√3): n=4, 2+√3 = 3.732 · Q(√2): n=6, (1+√2)² = 5.828 · Q(√5): n=7, φ⁴ (hat) · Q(√15): n=8 (Spectre) ·
Q(√6): n=10 · Q(√13): n=11, β² = 10.908 (β bronze).

**Lemma A (no single-cluster collapse).** If a tiling by two prototiles has an irrational frequency ratio
(Ammann–Beenker squares:rhombs = √2, Penrose thick:thin = φ), it cannot be partitioned into copies of one cluster of
its own tiles: every copy has the same rational composition, so the ratio would be rational. Hence a silver monotile
cannot be "a patch of Ammann–Beenker"; the irrational must live in orientation or neighbourhood frequencies, as in the
hat (unreflected:reflected → φ⁴ with all hats congruent).

**Lemma B (deformation keeps the field; standard, cite before use).** Shape changes that keep the combinatorics
(the hat's Tile(a,b) family, the Spectre's curved edges) give homeomorphic tiling spaces with the same substitution
matrix, hence the same inflation and field. So deforming the hat can never reach √2, √3 or √13: route 4a is closed for
this question; a new field needs new combinatorics (route 4b, structure first).

**What the searches answer so far.** Kite grid, n ≤ 10: 13,212 shapes; the only open ones are the hat and the turtle
(golden). So no small polykite monotile exists for any other field. Silver / √3 / bronze remain open; the next test is
structure-first: search small substitutions with these sibling inflations and ask whether one shape realises them.

## 14. One axis at a time: Beatty partitions vs Frobenius semigroups

- Scope: a tiling is a (generalised) cut-and-project set iff it has pure point order (Baake–Lenz–Moody).
  Rational slope gives a periodic tiling, irrational slope a quasiperiodic one. Pinwheel (not Pisot) is not a model set;
  the chair and Socolar–Taylor use a 2-adic internal space.
- Reduction to 1D: de Bruijn's pentagrid makes Penrose the dual of 5 line families; along each family the tile
  sequence is Sturmian (Fibonacci word, slope 1/φ: Conway worms).
- Irrational slope: Beatty sequences ⌊nα⌋, ⌊nβ⌋ with 1/α + 1/β = 1 partition N exactly (Rayleigh).
  Golden: 1,3,4,6,8,9,11,12… and 2,5,7,10,13… (Wythoff).
- Rational slope a/b: Christoffel words and the semigroup ⟨a,b⟩; Frobenius number ab − a − b;
  Sylvester: n is representable iff ab − a − b − n is not. Example ⟨3,5⟩: gaps 1,2,4,7.
- A 2D tiling = several 1D slope problems that must agree where line families cross.
- Field fingerprint test: read the 1D tile sequence along ribbons and fit its Sturmian slope. A silver candidate must
  show a slope in Q(√2).

## 15. Hierarchy first (Razvan, 2 Nov 02:17: "Penrose kites are level 0 of a higher hierarchy; find that structure")
Hat metatile substitution, read from our hat code (rows = new cluster, columns = H, T, P, F inside it):
  H' = 3H + 1T + 3P + 3F,  T' = 1H,  P' = 2H + 1P + 2F,  F' = 2H + 1P + 3F.  Perron eigenvalue φ⁴ = 6.854.
  Hats per level-1 cluster (H, T, P, F) = (4, 1, 2, 2); H-cluster ladder 4, 25, 169, 1156, 7921 = F(2k+1)².
Silver programme: (1) a recipe matrix with Perron eigenvalue a power of 1+√2 (e.g. x² − 6x + 1 factor);
(2) one shape S whose copies fill every level-1 cluster type, with the clusters composing consistently level after level.
Step (2) is the hard, geometric part (the hat's clusters change shape between levels and only converge in the limit).
Lemma A forces the √2 into cluster counts/orientations, never into a mix of two fixed piece types.
Data step run first: near misses (periodic only with ≥ 8 tiles per block, or stuck only at disc radius ≥ 4) on the
45° grid, the closest periodic relatives of an aperiodic tile, to look for clusters that repeat at two scales.

## 16. Building the hierarchy search: what the clusters must look like (2 Nov, 02:40)
Natural construction tried first: deform the silver (Ammann–Beenker) tiling onto the 45° grid. Its edge star can be any
e0=(a,0), e1=(b,b), e2=(0,c), e3=(−d,d) with a,b,c,d positive integers (all six dets ab, ac, ad, bc, 2bd, cd > 0), so the
deformed tiling stays valid and every tile becomes a lattice parallelogram with an integer area (the area ratio √2 that
blocked dissection disappears). Then look for one shape S that dissects every tile type.
Lemma C (kills it): if any union of copies of S tiles the plane periodically, so does S (cut every copy the same way).
Every parallelogram tiles periodically, and so does any tile whose opposite edges are translates (bumped edges included).
So no exact dissection of a deformed silver tiling, bumped or not, can give an aperiodic monotile.
Consequence: every cluster type at every level must be unable to tile on its own periodically, so clusters must carry
matching rules in their outline (the hat's T cluster is a single hat; its H, P, F clusters interlock).
Next build: the silver tiles WITH matching rules (Ammann's A5 set: arrowed edges, with the square marked) turned into
jigsaw pieces on the 45° grid (arrow = asymmetric bump), then a SAT search for one shape S that dissects every jigsaw piece,
then the sieve on S. Needs the exact A5 / Socolar arrow data from a primary source before coding.

## 18. Clustering = adding generators (Razvan, 2 Nov 03:04)
With edge generators e_k at equal angles (e_{k+n} = −e_k), the neighbours sum to a multiple of the middle one:
e_{k−1} + e_{k+1} = 2cos(θ)·e_k. So inflation (multiplication by λ) is pure addition of generators, an integer matrix:
- silver, 45° star (4 gens): (1+√2)·e_k = e_{k−1} + e_k + e_{k+1}; M = I + S + S⁻¹, eigenvalues 2.414 (×2), −0.414 (×2).
- golden, 36° star (5 gens): φ·e_k = e_{k−1} + e_{k+1}; M = S + S⁻¹, eigenvalues 1.618 (×2), −0.618 (×2), −2 (rational part).
- 12-fold, 30° star (6 gens): (2+√3)·e_k = e_{k−1} + 2e_k + e_{k+1}; eigenvalues 3.732 (×2), 0.268 (×2), 2 (×2, rational part).
(computed with numpy.) The large eigenvalue stretches physical space; its Galois conjugate (|·| < 1) shrinks the internal
space: the Pisot condition is exactly what keeps the window finite. Every available inflation is an integer polynomial in
S + S⁻¹, so λ ∈ Z[2cos(π/n)]: the finite-generator symmetry picture from section 3, now with a concrete integer matrix.
A hierarchy-first search fixes M first; the cluster counts then follow from M acting on the generators.

## 19. The ladder as linear terms in φ, and the metallic generalisation (Razvan, 2 Nov 07:18)
φ² = φ + 1 gives φ^(2n) = F(2n−1) + F(2n)·φ (1+φ, 2+3φ, 5+8φ, 13+21φ). The hat's H-cluster ladder 4, 25, 169, 1156, 7921 = F(2k+1)²
obeys a(k+1) = 7·a(k) − a(k−1) − 2, with 7 = φ⁴ + φ⁻⁴ (checked). Silver analogue: odd Pell numbers squared 1, 25, 841, 28561, 970225
obey x(k+1) = 34·x(k) − x(k−1) − 8, ratio → (1+√2)⁴ = 33.97, with 34 = (1+√2)⁴ + (1+√2)⁻⁴ (checked). Every metallic mean μ (μ² = nμ + 1)
gives the same arithmetic: μ^(2k) is linear in μ with generalised-Fibonacci coefficients, and squares of alternate terms satisfy a
three-term recurrence with trace μ⁴ + μ⁻⁴. So the counting side of the hat replicates in every metallic family.
Caveat from the data: the Spectre's field Q(√15) contains no metallic mean (n² + 4 = 15k² has no solution); its inflation 4+√15 is a
sibling unit (x² − 8x + 1). Both known monotiles have sibling (norm +1) area inflations; metallic squares are siblings, but siblings
are the wider family. The geometric side (one shape realising the ladder) is the open part.
